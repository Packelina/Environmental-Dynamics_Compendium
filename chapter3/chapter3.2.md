# 3.2. Classification of estuaries

There are several different approaches to classify estuaries. One is to consider the geomorphological origin and geological formation for a classification primarily based on topographical features. Another approach is to investigate the salinity structure and thereby the stratification characteristics of an estuary, giving a classification based on internal circulation patterns. The distinction between the different approaches is by no means clear, since the topographical features can significantly affect the flow characteristics and mixing parameters.

(sec-3-2-1)=
## 3.2.1. Topographical and morphological classification

The topographical form of an estuary can play a significant role for the estuarine exchange flows and mixing characteristics<!-- CHANGE: "caracteristics" -> "characteristics" -->. Estuaries have an age, since they originate from the time of the last glaciation. At the last glacial peak (approx. 20 000 years ago) the global sea level was around 120 meters below the present due to the vast volumes of water trapped in the ice sheets. Thus the present estuaries are located in ancient river valleys and generally the erosion and sedimentation rates are not in steady state. The relative age, or maturity, of an estuary is then a result of the sedimentation rate within the estuary and the erosion rate from coastal processes, such as tides and waves. Depending of the material transport rate in the river estuaries can be in a transient phase, where the net flux of sediments into the estuary is positive, or in a relative stationary mode. Thus the estuaries are classified in a scale loosely based on their relative age and their maturity. In this group we find

- Coastal-plain estuaries - are basically drowned river valleys where the present sedimentation rate is not in pace with the post-glacial inundation. The bathymetry is still similar to the ancient river valley and sand bars and banks are still developing. The relative age is then quite low.
- Fjords - formed from ice age valley glaciers that broadened and deepened the valley floor. At the mouth large amount of sediments (terminal moraines) were deposited creating a very shallow sill into the fjord when the glaciers retreated. Some sills are not deeper than a couple of meters whereas the depth within the fjords usually ranges up to a couple of hundred meters. Since the sediment flux is usually very low, the bathymetry very deep, the fjords are generally assumed to have a very low relative age
- Bar-built estuaries - are strictly speaking also drowned river valleys but in this case the sedimentation rate is in pace with the inundation, creating a more mature estuarine type. Across the mouth of the estuary is a bar where waves break and that is formed by deposited sediments. The estuary is in steady state in the sense that even if occasional storm events break the bar a similar one is quickly created after the event. This type of estuary is associated with tropical climate and rivers with high sediment loads.

(sec-3-2-2)=
## 3.2.2. Classification from salinity structure

Since salt water is heavier than freshwater, the lighter freshwater will tend to float on top of the salt sea water. The degree of mixing between the two water columns within the estuary defines the salinity structrure and plays an important role in the characteristics of internal water exchange and circulation. Important processes for internal mixing of different waters are e.g. tides, upwelling and sea set-up. Certain degrees of stratification, on the other hand, inhibit circulation in the whole water body, creating stagnant zones that may lead to accumulation of nutrients and pollutants, high decomposition rates of organic material and eventually oxygen depletion.

- Salt wedge type estuaries - are the typical example of highly stratified estuaries, where the lighter freshwater floats on top of the denser saltwater. The vertical salinity distribution (and thus density) has a very sharp jump at the depth of the interface between the two layers. This density interface is called the *halocline* and is comparable to the seasonal thermocline in lakes. The halocline, however, is permanent throughout the year. The result is a stratification in the water column where the mixing between the layers is small. However small, there will nevertheless still be a raise of salinity in the top layers along the estuary, salt water has to mix with fresh water to some degree. The salt is taken from the bottom layer and the loss of water in the bottom column has to be compensated by inflow of salt water from the sea. Thus a transport of saltwater from the sea into the estuary, with a corresponding mixing is induced.
- Fjord type estuaries - are also highly stratified and similar to the salt wedge estuaries. However, the lower, salt layer is often very deep, because of the topographical form of fjords. The top layer is often of constant depth from the head to the mouth of the fjord and saltwater inflow is controlled at the mouth by the flow over a shallow sill. Thus the flow characteristics of the inlet/outlet can be very different from the internal circulation. As the outflowing freshwater meets the ocean and the tidal waves, very strong currents are often seen over the sill. Inside the fjord, though, long periods of stagnation within the bottom layer are not uncommon, with oxygen depletion in the lower water body as a consequence.
- Partially mixed estuaries - exists where the tidal range is sufficiently large to produce turbulent mixing between the two layers in the estuary. The tidal current that is oscillating back and forth along the estuary will produce eddies in the interface between the layers. These eddies mix the water in both directions, raising the salinity in the upper layer, and lowering in the bottom layer. Thus, the salinity gradient is less steep than in the highly stratified estuary but due to the salt balance there still has to exist a two-layer flow in the estuary with seaward flowing top layer and a landward bottom current of sea water.
- Vertically homogeneous (mixed) estuaries - can at least theoretically form when the tidal currents are so strong that they completely mix the water along the whole water column. Therefore the vertical salinity differences become negligible. However, a longitudinal salinity gradient exists from head to mouth of the estuary.
- Inverse estuaries - are also called a negative estuaries. If the evaporation rate is higher than the freshwater influx (river and precipitation) the salt concentration in the surface water will increase and the saltier surface water sinks towards the bottom. This will induce an inverse two-layer transport of water, where sea water is flowing *into* the estuary in the top layer and heavier, saltier water will flow *out* of the estuary along the bottom.
