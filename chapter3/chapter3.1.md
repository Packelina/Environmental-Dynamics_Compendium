# 3.1. Introduction

Generally, coastal zones and the continental shelves (the shallower ocean zone, stretching approximately 200 km out from the coast) are characterized by very high biological production, thus being economically important as well as providing a high environmental value in terms of biodiversity and recreational potential. Coastal waters (including the shelves) supply for about 90 percent of the global fish catch @wolanski2004.

Estuaries are formed in the narrow boundary zones between land and ocean, at mouth of rivers and other fresh-water discharge points. One classical definition of an estuary is provided by @cameron1963.

*An estuary is a semi-enclosed coastal body of water which has a free connection with the open<!-- CHANGE: "...the op en"--> sea and within which sea water is measurably diluted with fresh water derived from land drainage.*

A general sketch of the estuarine system is shown in {ref}`fig_estuaries_general_sketch`, highlighting the dominant pathways, interactions and processes within the estuary.

Circulation in estuaries is forced by tides, density differences due to temperature and salinity gradients, waves, upwelling, wind, precipitation, evaporation and of course riverine inflow. Estuaries recieves water and material input from the sea and from the upstream river catchment. Since the esturary functions as a sieve for river water, the impacts on the relatively small estuary from the river catchment, which usually is several times larger than the estuary can be severe. Many estuaries are today exposed to high stress levels, that often can be derived from inland activities and not only activities within the actual estuary. This stress can give degraded water quality, lowered production and human health issues. The result is estuaries that are no longer the high quality producing environments that originally was the reason for people settling down there. Reasons for this are several, including sedimentation due to deforestation and change in land use within the catchment, overfishing and trawling, destruction of wetlands, eutrophication, pollution, dams and dykes.

:::{note} Did you know?
Eutrophication of coastal systems is a world-wide phenomenon. Dead bottom zones have spread exponentially since the 1960s and today more that 400 coastal systems are affected by hypoxia and the total area is estimated to 245 000 square kilometers. The world's largest hypoxic zone is found in the Baltic Sea, covering an area of approximately 100 000 square kilometers.
:::
