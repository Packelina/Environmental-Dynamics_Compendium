# 1.1. Quantifying environmental change

(sec-1-1-1)=
## 1.1.1. Sustainable development: Sustainability of what, where and for whom?

The natural and human environments are presently experiencing rapid changes, related to economic growth, pressures on natural resources and changing environmental conditions. These changes will pose great challenges to ecosystems and human societies in the future. Looking at these issues in a somewhat abstract manner, we can summarize the currently most burning issues of the societies by the following "equation":

:::{math}
:enumerated: false
\text{Economic growth} + \text{Sustainable development} + \text{Security} = ?
:::

where "economic growth" should be understood in a global sense, i.e., economic growth of rich and poor societies; thus, this equation is also relevant for reducing poverty. As is well known, the contrast in income is staggering, from \$4/day for almost two billion people to more than \$400/day (or even far more) for industrialized societies. A higher income in a society traditionally implies higher turnover of material and energy. The turnover of material and energy by humans is already very large and in some cases comparable to the material and energy turnover in nature. Further increase of income/growth on the global scale will clearly increase the already high global pressure on natural systems. It is imperative that all societies set rules, directions and constraints that "navigate" any current and future economic growth to a more sustainable governance and management of natural resources.

Sustainable development is a broad, accepted goal of most societies. It has been formulated as a goal in the best long-term interest of the modern, industrialized society/civilization. Although the goal is understood intuitively and in a general sense, its operational, engineering implications and modes are far from clear. For instance, sustainability of what - resources, economy, ecosystems, or all together, but to what extent for each? Are we referring to the entire planet, or regional/local sustainability, i.e., what kind of spatial connotation does "sustainable development" have, how are the local and global to be reconciled? Finally, who is the "bearer" of such development anywhere, who sets the criteria and in whose interest is it anyway?

The answers to the above questions are subjective, not objective. That is, the meaning attached to "sustainable development" depends on the values of the person defining it. Clearly, not everybody shares the same values. How is this to be reconciled in practice? These are very difficult questions which are in a slow process of being answered. As future engineers, you need to gain a good understanding of the basic issues and principles that govern sustainable development, in order to take an active part and drive the progress toward a more sustainable society, based to the extent possible on "facts and figures".

(sec-1-1-2)=
## 1.1.2. Setting technology and growth in a common context

Technology/engineering has been and is instrumental for economic growth and wealth increase. Whereas initially technology essentially implied "tools" for achieving certain simple goals, like an axe or a mechanical digger, in current terms technology is an all-embracing part of society, linked in many ways with all human activities and to an increasing degree also influencing natural systems. Clearly, much effort over the past decades has been to understand implications of technology, i.e., how it is set in a broader human and natural context. In spite of this effort, there are still many open issues. Above all, the basic understanding of the link between technical, natural and socio-economic systems is the key to providing a direction toward a more sustainable society. In fact, a suitable analogy is sailing where we are navigating in a certain direction under constantly changing conditions. As engineers, irrespective of particular field, we have the responsibility to understand the role of technology in a broad context, and constantly improve our navigation tools toward more eco-efficient and sustainable development.

The trustworthy "navigation tool" will require establishing/understanding operational links. Of particular importance are the constraints which have to be imposed, for instance on technology, with respect to a set of criteria for which a broad consensus has been reached. We need to work together in setting these constraints, and in general maintaining technology as a positive force for the restoration and preservation of ecosystems.

A "checklist" of a few important issues includes the following:

- Setting constraints for development; proposing them and reaching a consensus with society/stakeholders.
- Follow-up/monitor so that we can all agree we are moving in the "right" direction
- Constantly update the constraints/criteria based on new knowledge/information. 

Clearly information flow is very important.

(sec-1-1-3)=
## 1.1.3. Emergence of a new discipline: _Meta-engineering_
Navigating towards a more sustainable society implies establishing new links on a variety of levels between technical, natural and socio-economic systems, in space and/or time. Thus the overall complexity of systems required for successful "navigation" is increasing. However, the key point is that irrespective of the level of complexity or particular issue at hand, we need to work/establish quantitative criteria since only these can be objectively compared, even if establishing these criteria will inevitably be based on subjective judgments and values. From an operational/engineering viewpoint, we can list several "difficulties" in establishing quantitative criteria. 

- Overwhelming complexity
- Dynamics
- Uncertainty
- Optimization
- Sensitivity/risk
- Information

## 1.1.4. Scope of the course
This course is focused on providing the basis for quantifying environmental change, as induced by anthropogenic activities. In other words, linking technology/engineered systems with natural systems.

This course is focused mainly of the physical aspects of environmental change. Traditionally, physics is all about generality, whereas chemistry and biology are all about the specific (compounds, reactions, species). Biological and chemical reactions are also considered in the course howbeit in a general quantitative way; specifics of chemistry and biology are dealt with in other courses.

Also, we do not focus on specific technologies and specific engineered systems, although we provide principles applicable to such systems. Energy, for instance, is primarily related to engineered systems and linked to technology for its generation, conversion and transfer. Thus we treat energy only briefly by stating the main principles and considering its flow and transformation in a few specific contexts (e.g., climate change); there are other texts that give much more on energy. Also, we do not address measurement techniques, which is also a technological field; this too is available in other texts/courses. Hence there is no discussion on the important issues of noise and radiation; the focus in this course is mainly on material flows.
