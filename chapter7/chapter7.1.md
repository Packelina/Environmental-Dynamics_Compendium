(sec-7-1)=
# 7.1. Introduction - The dynamic nature of human impacts on the environment

(sec-7-1-1)=
## 7.1.1. A historical view

A human being demands *goods* (materials/substances) for his/her need for energy, proteins and minerals to live and grow. In prehistoric times these goods were directly obtained from the nearby surroundings. As natural supplies were limited, goods have been subsequently obtained by using the natural resources from the environment. The material turnover of goods has changed dramatically from prehistoric to modern times due to steadily increasing population and living standards; as the input of goods has increased by more than an order of magnitude also the emissions have increased. One of the important differences of modern households compared to prehistoric times is the very large and still increasing accumulation of materials in the anthroposphere in form of cars, buildings, furniture, appliances etc.

Human consumption of goods and the associated waste production leaves heavy footprints on the whole planet. Human civilization has always changed the face of the Earth, however, until very recently natural material/ substance flows were always dominant.

(sec-7-1-2)=
## 7.1.2. Humans impact on the environment

In a sustainable society anthropogenic mass fluxes do not alter global natural cycles neither do they alter local environmental qualities. The high input and output flows of modern society as shown in the figure above however imply direct and indirect impacts on the environment. The most obvious one may be the production of waste which implies a direct pollution of water, air and soil by waste disposal/sewage. But also the high consumption of end user goods requires processing of natural materials which also generates wastes, e.g. disposal of heavy metal enriched by products of the mining industry. Further, the high accumulation of materials within our society may have significant impacts on the long-term pollution of the environment which are yet to be investigated.

Examples of negative impacts of anthropogenic flows are many. The magnitude of the flow of materials undergoing the global sedimentary cycle (of rock weathering, erosion, transport, forming of sediments and rockforming processes) is about 10 billion t/yr, while anthropogenic flows (caused by agriculture, mining, building and construction, industrial production, the generation of wastes and their disposal) are at least ten times larger. Some of these anthropogenic flows are very dangerous, such as the erosion of topsoil (which amounts to 30-40 billion t/yr) or man-made additions to the biogeochemical cycles of carbon, nitrogen and other biogenic as well as toxic elements. Natural flows of copper, silver, lead, mercury and other heavy metals are only tiny fractions one hundredth to less than one thousandth - of the corresponding anthropogenic flows (see e.g. {ref}`fig_cadmium_flows`).

:label: fig_cadmium_flows
:width: 500px

In general, man-made material flows may have significant impacts on natural flow cycles if they exceed natural flows as this may disturb the balanced state of systems. The excess loads force the system to achieve a new equilibrium. As the example of the release of Greenhouse gases from the anthroposphere to the atmosphere seems to indicate such dynamics illustrates, such dynamics may have far reaching consequences for our society. It is therefore important to investigate the anthropogenic metabolism and its connections/interferences with the natural environment represented by the hydro-, bio- and atmosphere by means of static and dynamic modeling.
