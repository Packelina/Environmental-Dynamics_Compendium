(sec-6-3)=
# 6.3. Climate change

The following section discusses the impact of humans on the climate system. Humans have always influenced their environment. The total effects, implications and responses of the climate system on these anthropogenic influence are, however, not predictable with certainty due to the dynamic nature of the climate system.

:::{note} Did you know?
Coincidences or indications of a trend? The hottest year in the hottest decade of the hottest century of the last millennium was 1998. The ten warmest years since 1860 have all been since 1980. The sea level has risen between 10 and 25 cm because of thermal expansion of the oceans. Mount Kilimanjaro has lost 75% of its ice cap since 1912. The ice on Africa's tallest peak could vanish entirely within 15 years.
::: 

(sec-6-3-1)=
## 6.3.1. Observing climate change

One of the major problems scientists are facing today is to identify if our climate system is changing due to human impact. Advanced observation technologies, such as satellites, are quite new tools and cannot provide data for the past. In the same way, weather records extend back only about 130 years. Therefore, deep ice cores from glaciers and the polar ice caps, and the rings on trees are methods used to investigate past climates.

**The role of fossil fuels** - Fossil fuels have been used as power source for more than hundred years, since then vast quantities of carbon dioxide have been released to the atmosphere. Since the industrial revolution, CO{sub}`2` concentrations worldwide have increased from approximately 280 ppm (or 0.028%) to around 365 ppm (0.0365%). This means that approximately 3 GigaTons CO{sub}`2` are released to the atmosphere every year. When discussing anthropogenic induced climate variation, it is understood that this huge emission will somehow have an impact on the natural climate system.

(sec-6-3-2)=
## 6.3.2. Human impact on the climate system

It is a fact that we change, among others, the atmospheric composition of the atmosphere. This implies an anthropogenic induced forcing on the climate system that, as discussed earlier, tries to reach a new equilibrium.

**Changes in the atmospheric composition** - The carbon dioxide concentration in the atmosphere is currently at the highest estimated level in 400 000 years (about 359 ppmv (parts per million by volume)) and all trends indicate a further increase due to man-made emissions mainly from the combustion of fossil fuels and forests. Also other atmospheric components, such as nitrous oxide and methane, are increasingly released to the atmosphere ({ref}`fig_human_influence_atmosphere`). Reasons are agricultural, industrial and other activities.

:::{figure} ../images/fig_human_influence_atmosphere.jpg
:label: fig_human_influence_atmosphere
:width: 600px
Indicators of the human influence on the atmosphere (@ipcc2001).
:::

The emission of gases such as nitrogen oxides (NO and NO{sub}`2`) and carbon monoxide (CO) have led to an increase in tropospheric ozone (O{sub}`3`), which acts as a greenhouse gas at this height. Chlorofluorocarbons and some other anthropogenic introduced halogen compounds have a depleting effect on the stratospheric ozone layer, while they are also strong greenhouse gases.

The amount of aerosol in the atmosphere is increasing caused by the emission of, e.g., mineral dust, sulphates and nitrates and soot from human industrial, energy-related, and land-use activities. The increase in greenhouse gas concentrations and aerosol content in the atmosphere results in a change in the radiative forcing to which the climate system must act to restore the radiative balance.

:::{note} Did you know?
The United States constitutes only 4% of the world population but is responsible for 1/4 of all CO{sub}`2` emissions, which implies an average of 18 ton of CO{sub}`2` is released by each US citizen every year. This is the highest of any country in the world, and more than China, India and Japan combined. Despite having just 2% of known oil reserves, the US consumes 25% of the world's oil production. 16% of world oil production goes into American cars alone.
::: 

**The resulting enhanced greenhouse effect** - As mentioned, except for stratospheric ozone, many greenhouse gas concentrations have increased since the beginning of the Industrial Revolution. This implies that more outgoing terrestrial radiation is trapped in the atmosphere, leading presumably to an increase in surface temperature (actual temperature $T_{\mathrm{a}}$). The depletion of the ozone layer, which implies that more terrestrial radiation can leave the atmosphere, partly balances this warming. As you may now understand, the response of the climate system on changes of the atmospheric composition is thus very complex. This is explained by feedback mechanism (see {ref}`Section 6.2.2. <sec-6-2-2>`) and uncertainties in our understanding related to all the involved processes, e.g., clouds and their interactions with radiation. As {ref}`fig_temperature_prediction_model` illustrated, there are vast differences in the prediction of future climate change and associated global warming. Considering for instance that the global mean temperature difference from the last Ice Age to the present is about 5 or 6°C one can however understand the magnitude of human impact on climate change.

**The effect of aerosols** - Besides greenhouse gases, aerosols as for instance sulphates, ashes and soots have the potential to significantly influence the earth's energy budget. They have both positive and negative effects on the energy budget as they reflect and absorb solar radiation. They further modify the formation an characteristics of clouds (changing cloud albedo). The impact of aerosols is often considered to only affect the local climate because the very short lifetime of aerosols and thus the ability of their removal by rain. Aerosols however have caused a worldwide darkening of the atmosphere also called **"global dimming"** that implies that less sunlight hits the ground, less energy absorption and therefore a lower global warming effect than without aerosols in the atmosphere. Therefore, some scientists warn about measures focusing on cleaning the air from aerosols without simultaneous reduction of greenhouse gases, as we would then see the "true" effect of global warming. It is believed that the heat waves 2002/2003 in Middle Europe causing several deaths have been intensified by extensive cleaning of the atmosphere from aerosols starting some years earlier.

**On the Impact of Land-Use Change** - The management of land involves a land-use change as a result from various competing human activities such as agriculture, forestry (reforestation and afforestation), urban areas and transportation. This change implies that the physical and biological properties of the land surface and thus the climate system are altered. As example we just need to consider biological processes of the terrestrial vegetation, which may lead to changes in the sources and sinks of the carbon cycle presented in the carbon cycle example. Other impacts on the climate system may be, for example, changes in land surface albedo and the exchange of water and gases between land and atmosphere.

(sec-6-3-3)=
## 6.3.3. Climate models for the projection of anthropogenic climate change

Climate models use simplified descriptions of the processes in and between the different components of the climate system. They may further include various feedback mechanisms. The ultimate objective of climate models is to understand the key physical, chemical and biological processes which govern climate. There exists a wide range of climate models from simple calculations of the radiative temperature that treat the earth as a single point (as our albedo model in {ref}`Section 6.2.1. <sec-6-2-1>`). Those models may also be expanded vertically (radiative-convective models), or horizontally (energy balance models). Finally, you can find (coupled) atmosphere-ocean-sea ice global climate models which discretisize and solve all motion equations. In addition, relatively simple "box models" can be used to describe flows across and within the different components of the climate system (see next section). Common for all model types is that basic laws and relationships of the climate system are expressed as a series of equations. These equations may be empirical, theoretical variable relationships or combinations of the two.

**Simple Global Carbon Box Model (SGCBM)** - The SGCBM uses a simple direct relationship of carbon in form of atmospheric CO{sub}`2` and global temperature based on the known history of atmospheric CO{sub}`2` and global temperature. 320 ppm (parts per million) is the initial concentration of CO{sub}`2` in the atmosphere, representing the preindustrial world. The dynamic concentration of atmospheric CO{sub}`2` is determined using a simplified version of the global carbon cycle ({ref}`fig_carbon_cycle`) considering the main carbon reservoirs of this cycle as boxes (Gt C) and processes that increase or decrease the carbon concentration in a component as fluxes (Gt C/y) into and out of the component, respectively ({ref}`fig_SGCBM`).

:::{figure} ../images/fig_SGCBM.png
:label: fig_SGCBM
:width: 400px
Compartments of the simple global carbon box model with information about reservoirs (GigaTons C) and fluxes among the different compartments (GigaTons C/yr) (@kwon1994). A conceptual representation of the actual carbon cycle as presented in {ref}`fig_carbon_cycle`.
:::

The three main reservoirs are the atmosphere, the biota and the oceans. The biota and the ocean pools are further divided into land biota and soil/detritus, and warm and cold surface water/biota and intermediate/deep water, respectively. Since photosynthesis and biota respiration are sensitive to temperature, a simple temperature feedback is implemented into the model (for detailed information about the various processes and parameterizations see @kwon1994).

The implemented processes of carbon flow among the compartments are:

- Terrestrial sphere: photosynthesis, plant respiration, litter fall, soil respiration and runoff
- Oceanic sphere: ocean atmosphere exchange, carbonate chemistry of seawater, advection, marine biota exchange, down- and upwelling, sedimentation, volcanism and metamorphism (geological process)
- Technosphere: fossil fuel burning and land-use changes (forest burning and soil disruption)

This model is a modified version of the Simple Global Carbon Model by @kwon1994. The model equations presented below describe the relationship between atmospheric CO{sub}`2` and the different fluxes between the components of the carbon cycle (numbers indicate the corresponding flows from compartment $i$ to compartment $j$ in {ref}`fig_SGCBM` with the 1st number as the source) and account for the dynamic equilibrium the carbon cycle is intended to reach in case of changes in any of the cycle components. The parameters and their values are described in the Appendix and on the special Simple Global Carbon Box Model (SGCBM) page[^SGCBM] (see also @kwon1994).

- **Global Temperature**

:::{math}
\Delta T = (c_{1} - 320) \times 0.01°C
:::

- **Atmosphere (Box 1)**

:::{math}
\begin{gathered}
\scriptsize \text{Variation of carbon mass in the atmosphere} = \text{Fossil fuel burning} + \text{Deforestation} - \text{Photosynthesis} \\
\scriptsize + \text{Plant respiration} + \text{Soil respiration} \pm \text{Gas exchange} \\[2ex]
V_1 \frac{dc_1}{dt} = F(t) + D(t) - \underbrace{\left[ k_{\mathrm{p}} \frac{c_1}{K_{\mathrm{m}} + c_1} + c_2 A_2 \theta_{\mathrm{p}}^{\Delta T} \right]}_{1 \to 2} + \underbrace{\left[ k_{\mathrm{br}} \frac{c_1}{K_{\mathrm{m}} + c_1} c_2 A_2 \theta_{\mathrm{br}}^{\Delta T} \right]}_{2 \to 1} \\
+ \underbrace{\left[ k_{\mathrm{sr}} c_3 V_3 \theta_{\mathrm{sr}}^{\Delta T} \right]}_{3 \to 1} + \underbrace{\left[ k_l S_{\mathrm{wo}} (P_{\mathrm{wo}} - P_{\mathrm{atm}}) A_4 \right]}_{4 \to 1} - \underbrace{\left[ k_l S_{\mathrm{co}} (P_{\mathrm{atm}} - P_{\mathrm{co}}) A_6 \right]}_{1 \to 6}
\end{gathered}
:::

- **Land Biota (Box 2)**

:::{math}
\begin{gathered}
\scriptsize \text{Variation of carbon mass in the Land Biota} = -\text{Deforestation} + \text{Photosynthesis} \\
\scriptsize - \text{Plant respiration} - \text{Litter fall} \\[2ex]
A_2 \frac{dc_2}{dt} = \underbrace{\left[ k_{\mathrm{p}} \frac{c_1}{K_{\mathrm{m}} + c_1} c_2 A_2 \theta_{\mathrm{p}}^{\Delta T} \right]}_{1 \to 2} - \underbrace{\left[ k_{\mathrm{br}} \frac{c_1}{K_{\mathrm{m}} + c_1} c_2 A_2 \theta_{\mathrm{br}}^{\Delta T} \right]}_{2 \to 1} - \underbrace{\left[ k_{\mathrm{e}} \frac{c_1}{K_{\mathrm{m}} + c_1} c_2 A_2 \right]}_{2 \to 3} - D(t)
\end{gathered}
:::

- **Soil and Detritus (Box 3)**

:::{math}
\begin{gathered}
\scriptsize \text{Variation of carbon mass in the Soil and Detritus} = \text{Litter fall} - \text{Soil respiration} \\
\scriptsize - \text{Wash out and transport (runoff)} \\[2ex]
V_3 \frac{dc_3}{dt} = \underbrace{\left[ k_{\mathrm{e}} \frac{c_1}{K_{\mathrm{m}} + c_1} c_2 A_2 \right]}_{2 \to 3} - \underbrace{\left[ k_{\mathrm{sr}} c_3 V_3 \theta_{\mathrm{sr}}^{\Delta T} \right]}_{3 \to 1} - \underbrace{\left[ k_{\mathrm{w}} c_3 V_3 \right]}_{3 \to 4 \text{ \& } 3 \to 6}
\end{gathered}
:::

- **Warm Ocean Surface Water (Box 4)**

:::{math}
\begin{gathered}
\scriptsize \text{Variation of carbon mass in the Warm Ocean Surface Water} = -\text{Gas exchange} \\
\scriptsize - \text{Warm ocean biota photosynthesis} + \text{Warm ocean biota respiration} + \text{Upwelling} \\
\scriptsize - \text{Ocean circulations} + \text{Wash out and transport (runoff)} \\[2ex]
V_4 \frac{dc_4}{dt} = -\underbrace{\left[ k_l S_{\mathrm{wo}} (P_{\mathrm{wo}} - P_{\mathrm{atm}}) A_4 \right]}_{4 \to 1} - \underbrace{\left[ k_{\mathrm{pwo}} c_4 V_5 \right]}_{4 \to 5} \\
+ \underbrace{\left[ k_{\mathrm{rwo}} c_5 V_5 \right]}_{5 \to 4} + \underbrace{\left[ k_{\mathrm{up}} c_8 A_4 \right]}_{8 \to 4} - \underbrace{\left[ Q_{46} c_4 \right]}_{4 \to 6} + \underbrace{\left[ \frac{2}{3} k_{\mathrm{w}} c_3 V_3 \right]}_{3 \to 4}
\end{gathered}
:::

- **Warm Ocean Biota (Box 5)**

:::{math}
\begin{gathered}
\scriptsize \text{Variation of carbon mass in the Warm Ocean Biota} = \text{Warm ocean biota photosynthesis} \\
\scriptsize - \text{Warm ocean biota respiration} - \text{Warm ocean biota sinking} \\[2ex]
V_5 \frac{dc_5}{dt} = \underbrace{\left[ k_{\mathrm{pwo}} c_4 V_5 \right]}_{4 \to 5} - \underbrace{\left[ k_{\mathrm{rwo}} c_5 V_5 \right]}_{5 \to 4} - \underbrace{\left[ k_{\mathrm{swo}} c_5 A_4 \right]}_{5 \to 8}
\end{gathered}
:::

- **Cold Ocean Surface Water (Box 6)**

:::{math}
\begin{gathered}
\scriptsize \text{Variation of carbon mass in the Cold Ocean Surface Water} = \text{Gas exchange} \\
\scriptsize - \text{Cold ocean biota photosynthesis} + \text{Cold ocean biota respiration} - \text{Downwelling} \\
\scriptsize + \text{Ocean circulations} + \text{Wash out and transport (runoff)} \\[2ex]
V_6 \frac{dc_6}{dt} = \underbrace{\left[ k_l S_{\mathrm{co}} (P_{\mathrm{atm}} - P_{\mathrm{co}}) A_6 \right]}_{1 \to 6} - \underbrace{\left[ k_{\mathrm{pco}} c_6 V_7 \right]}_{6 \to 7} \\
+ \underbrace{\left[ k_{\mathrm{rco}} c_7 V_7 \right]}_{7 \to 6} - \underbrace{\left[ k_{\mathrm{dc}} c_6 A_6 \right]}_{6 \to 8} + \underbrace{\left[ Q_{46} c_4 \right]}_{4 \to 6} + \underbrace{\left[ \frac{1}{3} k_{\mathrm{w}} c_3 V_3 \right]}_{3 \to 6}
\end{gathered}
:::

- **Cold Ocean Biota (Box 7)**

:::{math}
\begin{gathered}
\scriptsize \text{Variation of carbon mass in the Cold Ocean Biota} = \text{Cold ocean biota photosynthesis} \\
\scriptsize - \text{Cold ocean biota respiration} - \text{Cold ocean biota sinking} \\[2ex]
V_7 \frac{dc_7}{dt} = \underbrace{\left[ k_{\mathrm{pco}} c_6 V_7 \right]}_{6 \to 7} - \underbrace{\left[ k_{\mathrm{rco}} c_7 V_7 \right]}_{7 \to 6} - \underbrace{\left[ k_{\mathrm{sco}} c_7 A_6 \right]}_{7 \to 8}
\end{gathered}
:::

- **Intermediate and Deep Ocean Water (Box 8)**

:::{math}
\begin{gathered}
\scriptsize \text{Variation of carbon mass in the Intermediate and Deep Ocean Water} = \text{Downwelling} - \text{Upwelling} \\
\scriptsize + \text{Warm ocean biota sinking} + \text{Cold ocean biota sinking} - \text{Deposition (burial)} \\[2ex]
V_8 \frac{dc_8}{dt} = \underbrace{\left[ k_{\mathrm{dc}} c_6 A_6 \right]}_{6 \to 8} - \underbrace{\left[ k_{\mathrm{up}} c_8 A_4 \right]}_{8 \to 4} \\
+ \underbrace{\left[ k_{\mathrm{swo}} c_5 A_4 \right]}_{5 \to 8} + \underbrace{\left[ k_{\mathrm{sco}} c_7 A_6 \right]}_{7 \to 8} - \underbrace{\left[ k_{\mathrm{sed}} c_8 A_8 \right]}_{\text{Ocean Bottom}}
\end{gathered}
:::

The simplicity of the model brings of course the advantage of modeling the in reality very complex carbon cycle and associated global warming. However, the simplicity also implies limitations of the model, which are identified as the linear relationship of atmospheric CO{sub}`2` and global temperature, thus neglecting the impact of other greenhouse gases and aerosols on global temperature. The model is 0-dimensional and therefore spatial differences are not considered. Furthermore, vegetation and water depth are assumed with average depths over the whole planet, and all plants are represented by C3 plants (originally about 95%). Except for fertilization of terrestrial biota (Michaelis-Menten kinetics) all processes are described by first-order kinetics. Nevertheless, the model, calibrated with a 32-year record (1958-1989) can give good indications about future global temperatures ({ref}`fig_atmospheric_CO2_scenarios`) for a variety of scenarios including an increase, stagnation and decrease of the atmospheric CO{sub}`2` concentration. The Simple Global Carbon Box Model (SGCBM)[^SGCBM] and a example file with default parameters[^SGCBM_example] can be found on the course homepage.

**Java Climate Model (JCM)** - In contrast to the SGCBM, the JCM is designed to explore many interacting processes simultaneously. It is therefore necessary to include an attribution calculation method that is robust to a variety of scenarios and nonlinear feedback processes. This makes the model much more complex, however the JCM's graphical interface makes it easy to test the sensitivity to many factors. The model helps to investigate uncertainties and sensitivities of various predictions and enables simple to complex visualizations of prediction results on global or regional scales.

The model includes advanced process descriptions and features in different modules: carbon cycle (multilayer ocean, chemistry and heat feedbacks, four-box biosphere), gases/aerosols (CO{sub}`2`, CH{sub}`4`, N{sub}`2`O and O{sub}`3` precursors, 26 F gases, various aerosols), radiative forcing, heat fluxes (up-welling-diffusion energy balance model), sea level rise (thermal expansion and ice-melt) and regional climate. The JCM is based on scientific formula from IPCC and can replicate IPCC predictions. In addition, the JCM includes emissions and socioeconomic modules to consider non-scientific effects such as environmental policies, International agreements (Kyoto), people behavior and responsibility, regional characteristics and costs. For more details about the different modules and other information about the JCM model see @matthews2004. The Global Climate Java Model[^global_climate_java_model] can be found on the course homepage.

The aim of mankind's future development should obviously be to avoid dangerous climate change, by stabilizing or reducing concentrations of greenhouse gases and aerosols. With JCM one can investigate a range of levels and pathways to stabilize the CO{sub}`2` concentration, forcing, or temperature directly. The model provides a number of predefined scenarios including several predictions of future emissions of greenhouse gases and the impact of international agreements on global warming and associated effects. {ref}`fig_graph_prediction_global_temperature` illustrates predictions of how global temperature and sea levels will develop if all nations that signed the Kyoto protocol ultimately realize the requirements of the protocol.

**Projections of climate change** - No one knows for sure what the climate will be in the future. We may have to accept a global warming caused by the use of fossil fuels, but the resulting implications for our planet remain unknown. This results in that the magnitude and the danger of global warming is debated by some actors claiming that we are not affecting the climate at all. According to them, emissions reducing efforts become therefore unnecessary and dangerous to economic growth and development.

We have by now understood that the climate system is a dynamic, non-linear complex system consisting of huge variabilities due to internal and external forcing. Past climates, including warm and glacial periods, however, have partly been reproduced by climate models which suggests their validity even in future projections. A non-linear system such as the climate system may also undergo rapid changes as a response to internal processes or rapidly changing external forcing. Most scientists however believe that the probability of such rapid changes is small.
