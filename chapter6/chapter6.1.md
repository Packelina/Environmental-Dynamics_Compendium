(sec-6-1)=
# 6.1. Introduction to the climate system

To understand global climate change and variability you first have to understand what global climate is, and how it functions. For this reason, the following sections will help you to understand the climate system and its components.

(sec-6-1-1)=
## 6.1.1. Climate

In general, climate is defined as the average weather over a long period of time. The climate in a given region is determined by both natural and anthropogenic (human-made) factors (see {ref}`Section 6.1.2. <sec-6-1-2>`). The (IPCC) glossary definition is: "Climate in a narrow sense is usually defined as the "average weather", or more rigorously, as the statistical description in terms of the mean and variability of relevant quantities over a period of time ranging from months to thousands or millions of years. The classical period is 30 years, as defined by the World Meteorological Organization (WMO). These quantities are most often surface variables such as temperature, precipitation, and wind. Climate in a wider sense is the state, including a statistical description, of the climate system." (@ipcc2001).

**About the difference in weather and climate** - It is impossible to exactly define a boundary of what climate and what weather is and it often depends on the context. However, generally the current atmospheric conditions, which occur in the troposphere or the lower part of the atmosphere, including temperature, precipitation, wind, and humidity at a given place can be defined as **weather**. In other words, what you see or measure, like taking a temperature reading, is called weather and it happens right now or is likely to happen tomorrow or in the very near future (max two months).

**Climate**, on the other hand, is the general weather conditions over a long period of time. If we consider as an example any given day in January in Sweden, it could be rainy in Stockholm but sunny and mild in Gothenburg, southern Sweden. In Luleå up in the north of Sweden, we are not surprised to see January newscasts about subzero temperatures and huge snow drifts. The climate for an area is often defined as the area's "average" weather, which considers temperature highs and lows and precipitation rates for the past decades. Sometimes the term "climate" is also used to describe large-scale weather patterns in time or space such as an 'Ice Age' climate or a 'tropical' climate. Observe that weather and climate phenomena and systems do not only exist on Earth, they can also exist on any planet with an atmosphere.

**Climate variables** - Weather/climate is driven by differences in energy received from the sun that heats different parts of Earth to different extents. Resulting temperature differences lead to the important global circulation of warm and cold ocean water, global wind, and indirectly all other weather phenomena. The climate variables that affect our daily life most directly are: average, maximum and minimum temperature, wind near the surface of the earth, precipitation in its various forms, humidity, cloud type and amount, and solar radiation. Therefore, such variables are permanently observed by a huge number of weather stations around the globe.

However, the climate system is also affected by other indirect factors such as the vertical structure of the atmosphere and by surface characteristics. Climate is determined by oceanic and atmospheric circulations, interactions of the atmosphere with oceans and land by factors such as albedo, vegetation and soil moisture. The earth's climate is affected by even more factors all influencing the radiative balance (see {ref}`Section 6.1.2. <sec-6-1-2>`): atmospheric composition, solar radiation or volcanic eruptions, etc.

(sec-6-1-2)=
## 6.1.2. The climate system

The global climate system may be seen as a result of interactions among the atmosphere, hydrosphere, the ice sheets (cryosphere), living organisms (biosphere), soils, sediments and rocks (geosphere) and human activities (technosphere) ({ref}`fig_sketch_global_climate_system`). Interrelations of flows and cycles of energy and matter among the various spheres are all important for the various processes in the atmosphere (where climate/weather is take place) and causes (and effects) of climate change. As already mentioned, the driving force of the climate system is the sun pumping solar energy into the system. This energy may be absorbed, reflected, stored, transformed, put to work, and finally released back into the outer space. The important parameter that determines whether our planet (or any other planet) becomes cooler, warmer, or stays the same is the balance between the incoming solar energy and the outgoing terrestrial (or planetary) energy (see the theoretical presentation of mass/energy balance laws in {ref}`Chapter 1 <sec-1>`).

:label: fig_sketch_global_climate_system
:width: 600px

:::

Simplified we can say that the earth is approximately in a steady state in terms of energy. This means that 100% of the solar energy that is absorbed by the earth is ultimately radiated back into space. This implies that there is no net energy surplus on earth as the amount of energy received approximately equals the amount given back to space. However, temporal and spatial changes in temperature exist and are very important for our climate/weather; some of which are natural, while others may be due to anthropogenic impacts on the system.

**Components of the climate system** - From {ref}`fig_sketch_global_climate_system` we can identify the major elements as the atmosphere, geosphere, cryosphere, hydrosphere, biosphere and technosphere, which can include land and resource uses. Any changes of these factors can cause local, regional, or even global changes in the climate.

- The **atmosphere** is the most unstable and rapidly changing, but also most central and important part of the climate system. It is more thoroughly described in the paragraph following this list. The atmosphere consists of air which is a mixture of different gases and particles. It enables life on earth and integrates all the different components of the climate system. The ability of the atmosphere to control the earth's energy budget is however the most important aspect if discussing climate change.
- The **hydrosphere** includes all liquid surface and subsurface water. This includes fresh water (rivers, lakes and groundwater), saline water (oceans) and brackish water (transitional zones in estuaries and coastal zones). The hydrological cycle describes how the runoff of precipitation from the land returns to the oceans where water evaporates thereby closing the cycle. This circulation stores and transports a large amount of energy and its various components (especially the oceans) dissolve and store great quantities of carbon dioxide (carbon cycle example). Ocean circulations, driven by the wind and by density contrasts caused by salinity and thermal gradients (the so-called thermocline circulation), is much slower than the atmospheric circulation. Oceans are often designated as a regulator of the earth's climate on the longer time-scale because they damp vast and strong temperature changes. They are also important when talking about natural climate variability ({ref}`Section 6.2. <sec-6-2>`).
- Ice sheets, continental glaciers and snow fields, the sea ice and the permafrost are called the **cryosphere**. The most important characteristic of this group with regard to the climate system is its high reflectivity (albedo) for solar radiation and its role in driving deep ocean water circulation. Ice sheets are identified as a potential source of sea level variation due to the fact that they store a large amount of water that can be releases in case of global warming.
- The **geosphere** comprises all soils, sediments and rocks of the Earth's land masses and of the continental and oceanic crust. The geosphere's main impact on the climate is on a very long time scale (tens of millions or even hundreds of millions of years). Tectonic processes change the shape of ocean basins and the size of continental mountain chains which may influence the energy transfers within and between the coupled components of the climate system. However, even on a much shorter time scales physical and chemical characteristics of the soil, such as moisture and gases exchange with the atmosphere and oceans, may have an impact on global climate. Volcanic eruptions may even affect the climate with a very short time period but may impact the system on very long time scale. In particular the release of carbon dioxide into the atmosphere and the emission of dust and aerosols can affect the energy budget and regulation of the global climate system.
- The marine and terrestrial **biospheres** have a major impact on the atmosphere's composition. Plants uptake and release of greenhouse gases through the photosynthetic process store significant amounts of carbon (especially forests) by the uptake and release of greenhouse gases through the photosynthetic process. But the biosphere plays not only a central role in the carbon cycle, it is further of great importance for many other gases, such as methane and nitrous oxide. Other biospheric emissions are the so-called volatile organic compounds (VOC) which may have important effects on atmospheric chemistry, on aerosol formation and therefore on climate. The biosphere further controls how much energy that is received from the Sun is returned to the atmosphere. The topography and vegetation, for instance, influences how much water is evaporated.
- The **technosphere** can affect all the listed natural spheres. The use of natural resources such as the burning of fossils fuels and clearing of forests increases not only the concentration of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere, it also affects how energy received from the Sun is returned to the atmosphere. The emission of aerosols and the continued release of greenhouse gases can influence energy absorption and reflection.

**The Atmosphere - central part of the climate system** - The atmosphere of Earth is the layer of gases surrounding the planet earth and retained by the earth's gravity. It contains about 4/5 of nitrogen and 1/5 of oxygen, and trace amounts of other gases. Some of the trace gases, although present in only minute amounts, are very important to life on Earth. In particular two, carbon dioxide and ozone, can have a large impact on atmospheric processes. Another gas, water vapor, exists in concentration varying from being almost non-existent over desert regions to about 4% over the oceans and is important for weather dynamics since it exists in gaseous, liquid, and solid phases and absorbs radiant energy from the earth (see next section). Generally speaking, the atmosphere protects life on Earth by absorbing ultraviolet solar radiation and reducing temperature extremes between day and night. The atmosphere has no real border to space, it simply becomes thinner and fades away. However, 3/4 of the atmosphere's mass is within 11 km altitude. The atmosphere is divided vertically into four layers based on temperature: the troposphere, stratosphere, mesosphere, and thermosphere ({ref}`fig_atmosphere_layers`). This compendium, however, focus primarily on the layer in which we live - the troposphere.

:::{figure} ../images/fig_atmosphere_layers.jpg
:label: fig_atmosphere_layers
:width: 500px
Temperature (solid line) and pressure (dashed line) variations, principal composition and penetration of incoming radiation in the different regions of the atmosphere.
:::

All of the earth's weather occurs in the troposphere, which extends from the earth's surface to an average of 15 km ({ref}`fig_atmosphere_layers`). The pressure in this atmospheric layer ranges from 1 000 to 200 millibars. The temperature generally decreases with increasing height up to the tropopause (top of the troposphere); this is close to 200 millibars. The temperature averages 15°C near the surface and -57°C at the tropopause. The layer ends at the point where temperature no longer decreases with height. The moisture concentration decreases with height up to the tropopause. The vertical characteristics of the atmosphere include that pressure and density decrease exponentially with height. The vertical gradient of pressure $p$ is defined by the hydrostatic equation $\partial{p}/\partial{z}= -p(z)g$ , with $z$ being the altitude and $g$ the gravitational constant.

**Interactions among the components** - The climate system is characterized by a number of physical, chemical and biological interactions between its various components, which both vary in space and time. This implies that the climate system becomes extremely complex. Despite the differences in composition, physical and chemical properties, structure and behavior of the different parts, all subsystems are interrelated by fluxes of mass, heat and momentum. This is illustrated by the following two examples, which are selected because of their relevance for this course.

**Example: Energy Heat Transfer** - The atmosphere and Earth's surface, including biosphere, geosphere, hydrosphere and cryosphere, are strongly coupled and exchange, among others, water vapor and heat through evaporation. These processes are part of the hydrological cycle and results in condensation, cloud formation, precipitation and runoff, and supply energy to the weather/climate system (see for example {ref}`fig_earth_energy_balance`). The energy transfer between the earth's surface and the atmosphere is partly based on infrared radiation, but energy is also transferred by conduction, convection, and radiation.

- **Radiation** from Earth's surface to the atmosphere is simply caused by Earth's temperature and is equal to 70% of total heat flux from surface to atmosphere. This thermal radiation does not require a medium, which means that it can travel through a vacuum like space. This is for example how solar energy reaches the earth, and how heat escapes from the earth back into space.
- **Convection** (also advection) is the transport of heat as a result of the movement of water being warmed. When water is warmed its atoms move faster and spread apart, reducing its density, i.e. it will rise. One could say "Heat rises" but it's actually the water that is rising. This creates convection currents and stands for the remaining 30% of total heat flux from surface to atmosphere. Convection (together with ocean currents) plays a significant role in transferring heat polewards and thereby distributing heat over the whole Earth. Air mass advection describes how either cold or warm air masses travel to areas of opposite temperature characteristics.
- **Conduction** describes the transfer of heat (kinetic) energy between two objects in physical contact. Soils are better conductors than water; water is a better conductor than air. Therefore, no significant amount of energy is transferred by conduction from Earth's surface to the atmosphere, only a thin layer of air touching the ground is warmed by conduction, which may however be important for the local weather/climate.

Energy that is transported by heat (convection and conduction) is often divided into two types:

- **sensible heat** heat that causes a change in temperature in an object; and
- **latent heat** heat that is absorbed from or released to the environment as a result of a phase change in water (thus, latent heat is stored heat).

The heat transfer from Earth's surface to the atmosphere is approximately 37 W/m{sup}`2` and 63 W/m{sup}`2` for sensible and latent heat, respectively.

The exchange of heat is associated with a changes of temperature and volume of the considered material (here air, soil or water) and is described by the first law of thermodynamics (see also section {ref}`Chapter 4 <sec-4>` in the main document):

:::{math}
dQ = M\left(C_{\mathrm{p}} dT - \frac{1}{\rho} dp\right), \quad C_{\mathrm{p}} = C_{\mathrm{v}} + R
:::

with $C_{\mathrm{p}}$ as the specific heat capacity of air at constant pressure, $C_{\mathrm{v}}$ as the specific heat capacity of air at constant volume and $R$ as the specific gas constant. Assuming constant pressure, isobaric heating and cooling of a mass $M$ of, e.g., air simplifies to:

:::{math}
dQ = MC_{\mathrm{p}} dT
:::

**Example: The Carbon Cycle and Atmospheric Carbon Dioxide** - Carbon in form of carbon dioxide (CO{sub}`2`) is one of the main greenhouse gases that has been identified to cause global warming. Carbon in its various forms cycles between the main reservoirs of its biogeochemical system: the atmosphere, hydrosphere, geosphere and biosphere ({ref}`fig_carbon_cycle`). Processes such as volcanic eruptions, respiration, soil processes, combustion of carbon compounds and oceanic evaporation release carbon from the various climate subsystems to the atmosphere. The main sinks of carbon from the atmosphere are the dissolution in oceans and the consumption during plant photosynthesis.

:::{figure} ../images/fig_carbon_cycle.png
:label: fig_carbon_cycle
:width: 500px
Diagram of the carbon cycle. The green numbers indicate how much carbon is stored in various reservoirs, in GigaTons of Carbon. The black numbers indicate how much carbon moves between reservoirs each year (Note that estimates for anthropogenic sources of CO{sub}`2` are usually accompanied with ranges of uncertainty as indicated in the figure; @uvic2005).
:::

Like other biogeochemical cycles, the global carbon cycle is in dynamic equilibrium, which means that it may change to a new equilibrium if any of the carbon flows in the cycle changes. To put some figures on some carbon flows we may look at the atmosphere that stores about 750 Gt (GigaTons) of carbon (in the form of CO{sub}`2`). The ocean represents an enormous storage of carbon, with over 38 000 Gt in the deep layers and about 1 000 Gt in the upper layers ({ref}`fig_carbon_cycle`). The regrowth of the Northern Hemisphere forests is considered as the only major anthropogenic sink of atmospheric CO{sub}`2`.
