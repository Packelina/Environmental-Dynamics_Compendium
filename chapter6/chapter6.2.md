(sec-6-2)=
# 6.2. Climate variations

Any change in the various components forming the climate system and/or the interactions between these components, and changes of external forcing (solar radiation), may result in natural climate variations. This should not be mistaken with climate change! The following two main sections introduce various aspects of natural climate variations, followed by an introduction to the human influence on the climate system.

(sec-6-2-1)=
## 6.2.1. Natural forcing of the climate system

**The global energy balance** - The Sun's hydrogen fusion creates an immense amount of energy, heating the sun's surface to around 6 000°K;. The sun then radiates energy outwards in the form of ultraviolet and visible light. Earth's incoming solar radiation amounts to 1 372 W/m{sup}`2` outside the earth's atmosphere (averaged throughout a year). The incoming solar radiation per m{sup}`2` of Earth's surface is then about 342 W/m{sup}`2` ({ref}`fig_earth_energy_balance`). To simplify we may say that the total amount of solar energy received by the earth is equal to 100 units.

:::{figure} ../images/fig_earth_energy_balance.png
:label: fig_earth_energy_balance
:width: 500px
The earth's annual and global mean energy balance. 49% of the incoming solar radiation (168 W/m{sup}`2`) are absorbed by the surface. That heat is returned to the atmosphere as sensible heat, as evapotranspiration (latent heat) and as thermal infrared radiation. Most of this radiation is absorbed by the atmosphere, which in its turn emits radiation both up and down (@ipcc2001).
:::

Of the incoming 100 units of solar energy, about 22 are immediately reflected by clouds and 9 are reflected and scattered back from the land surface and the atmosphere ({ref}`fig_earth_energy_balance`). All energy that enters and leaves the system has to be in balance as the system 'earth' is in a steady-state. The fraction of light that is reflected by Earth is called **Earth's albedo**, the fraction that is reflected by a specific surface is commonly referred to as only albedo. Clouds for example have an albedo of about 47%. In contrast, the earth's surface (dominated by water) reflects about 7% of the incident solar energy. Black materials have an albedo of 0 (no reflection) if they are perfectly black and perfectly white materials have an albedo of 1.0 (total reflection). (Test the interactive albedo/reflection applet[^albedo_applet] on the course homepage to understand the impact of surface type and thickness of clouds on local and therefore Earth's albedo.) The albedo also depends on the angle of the incoming radiation. The albedo of water, for instance, is relatively constant for an angle of 40° and higher relative to the horizon. For angles below 40°, the albedo of water increases significantly, which implies that it is about 0.5 at an angle of 10° and 1.0 at an angle of 0°.

The earth's energy balance is determined by the adsorbed solar and the outgoing terrestrial radiation. The absorbed solar energy is defined by the **albedo model** considering the earth's albedo ($\alpha_{\mathrm{Earth}} = 0.32$), the solar radiation right outside the atmosphere ($S = 1 \ 372$ W/m{sup}`2`) and the plane projection of Earth's surface area ($\pi R_{\mathrm{Earth}}^{2}$). On the other hand, the emitted energy from Earth to space is defined by the **black body model** using the earth's surface area ( $4\pi R_{\mathrm{Earth}}^{2}$ ), the Boltzmann constant ($\sigma = 5.67 \cdot 10^{-8}$ W/(m{sup}`2` K{sup}`4`)), which relates temperature to energy, and the earth's effective temperature ($T_{\mathrm{e,Earth}} = 255$°K). The global energy budget is then defined as:

:::{math}
S(1-\alpha_{\mathrm{Earth}})\pi R_{\mathrm{Earth}}^{2} = \sigma 4\pi R_{\mathrm{Earth}}^{2} T_{\mathrm{e,Earth}}^{4}
:::

The effective temperature of Earth accounts for the fact that our planet is not a perfect black body and therefore does not emit as much energy as would been associated with our actual temperature of $T_{\mathrm{a,Earth}} = 288$°K. {ref}`fig_different_wavelengths` shows the different wavelengths of incoming solar and outgoing terrestrial radiation together with energy emission spectra for a black body and Earth. As you can see, Earth's spectra has some wavelengths at which less energy is emitted to space ({ref}`fig_different_wavelengths`). These emission windows have the same wavelengths as the outgoing terrestrial radiation ({ref}`fig_different_wavelengths`). This implies that some terrestrial radiation is trapped in the atmosphere, which is commonly considered as the "Greenhouse effect".

:::{figure} ../images/fig_different_wavelengths.jpg
:label: fig_different_wavelengths
:width: 600px
Wavelengths of a) incoming solar and outgoing terrestrial radiation; and b) Blackbody emission spectra at (a) 240 K, (b) 280 and (c) the earth.
:::

**The natural greenhouse effect** - The most known greenhouse gases, carbon dioxide, water vapor (H{sub}`2`O), methane (CH{sub}`4`), nitrous oxide (N{sub}`2`O), and a few other gases have the ability to absorb heat. Against that, the major components of the atmosphere (H{sub}`2` and O{sub}`2`) do not absorb heat and therefore they do not contribute to the greenhouse effect.

:::{note} Did you know?
The greenhouse effect was first described by the Swedish scientist Svante Arrhenius in 1896. He theorized that doubling the amount of CO{sub}`2` in the atmosphere might raise the earth's temperature by 5 or 6°C. Arrhenius suspected that coal and fossil fuels burned by industrialized countries are adding to the levels of CO{sub}`2` in the atmosphere.
::: 

The heat-trapping ability of the greenhouse "Earth" is influenced by a number of factors including the transparency of the greenhouse cover (speak clouds), the reflection of energy from the surfaces inside the greenhouse (speak albedo), and the type of materials inside (speak energy transfer). The greenhouse effect describes the difference between the actual and the effective temperature, $T_{\mathrm{s}}$ - $T_{\mathrm{e}}$. As also many other planets have an atmosphere, the greenhouse effect is not only a phenomena of Earth. The planet Venus for example has a greenhouse effect of about 468°K compared to a greenhouse effect of 3°K on planet Mars. (Test the interactive $T_{\mathrm{e}}$ applet[^Te_applet] on the course homepage to investigate effective temperatures for the different planets of our solar system, or your own planet.)

**Radiative forcing and its variability** - We normally assume an equilibrium climate state, which implies that the average net radiation at the top of the atmosphere is zero. A change in either the incoming solar radiation and/or the emitted terrestrial radiation changes the net radiation. The corresponding imbalance is called "radiative forcing". External forcings, such as solar radiation or the release of aerosols by sand storms or volcanic eruption, may cause natural variations in the radiative forcing. This implies that the climate system must react to reach a new equilibrium. This includes, internal climate processes and feedbacks (see {ref}`Section 6.2.2. <sec-6-2-2>`), which may however also cause variations in the radiative balance by their impact on the reflected solar radiation or emitted infrared radiation.

(sec-6-2-2)=
## 6.2.2. Natural variability of climate

Radiative forcing and forcing variability as described in the previous section is only one cause of climate variability. Also the interactions among the various components of the climate system can cause a variability in climate on both global and regional scales.

**Causes of climate variability** - External induced climate variability can be orbit related, e.g. obliquity, eccentricity, precession and, as already discussed, solar variations. The atmosphere, i.e. the troposphere in our case, responses to this variability relatively fast (from days to weeks). Oceans need much longer response times (typically decades, centuries or millennium). The biosphere again responds much faster.

Besides the external forcing also internal forcing causes a natural variability in the climate system. As described in earlier sections, the various components of the climate system and their interactions are dynamic systems that respond in very different ways and on different time scales. Ocean circulation and changes in the atmospheric composition may be identified as the most significant internal forcing.

**Feedbacks** - Feedback are processes that either enhance or reduce a certain effect. Examples of positive and negative feedback mechanisms are:

- **the melting of ice and snow feedback** caused by global warming the melting of ice and snow covers, which are characterized by a very high albedo, reduce the global albedo, which amplifies the warming process as more solar energy is absorbed by the earth's surface
- **the evaporation feedback** an increase in temperature strongly increases the amount of evaporated water from land surface and oceans. This may cause an increased cloud formation and/or snow fall, which increases the global albedo and therefore reduces global warming.

**Variability on a global scale** - climate has varied naturally through all times. Glacial and non-glacial periods have occurred as long as earth has had a climate system similar to the one we have today. External forcing, as described above, are the main reasons for this variations.

**Variability on a regional scale** - regional variabilities in the climate/weather can be recognized in a much easier way than global variabilities. However, variabilities at one place are compensated by opposite variabilities at another place.
