(sec-5-1)=
# 5.1. Eutrophication

(sec-5-1-1)=
## 5.1.1. Introduction

Natural eutrophication is a process by which natural waters (like lakes) gradually age as a result from the accumulation of nutrients due to soil erosion and biological production. In this way waters will normally be filled with sediments over thousands of years.

The nutrient enrichment of natural waters contributed by man-made sources has become a matter of concern. Human activities have greatly accelerated the eutrophication (in some cases from centuries to decades!) increasing the rate at which nutrients enter the water body.

Lakes and large rivers can be classified according to their relative extent of eutrophication as:

- Oligotrophic
- Mesotrophic
- Eutrophic

An oligotrophic lake is a nutrient poor, a young lake where biological production is limited. An eutrophic lake is a nutrient rich, old lake where due to excessive addition of nutrients biological production is increased and the depth of water decreases. A mesotrophic lake lies somewhere in between and is characterized as a normal lake. A comparison between an eutrophic and oligotrophic lake is given in {ref}`fig_difference_lake_eutro_oligo` according to different characteristics like the shape of lakes, the concentrations of nutrients and chlorophyll-a (expressing a total phytoplankton content) in lakes.

:::{figure} ../images/fig_difference_lake_eutro_oligo.png
:label: fig_difference_lake_eutro_oligo
:width: 600px
The comparison between eutrophic and oligotrophic lakes (@kiely1997)
:::

(sec-5-1-2)=
## 5.1.2. Biological processes in aquatic ecosystems

(sec-5-1-2-1)=
### 5.1.2.1. How the chemical composition of natural waters is regulated?

In aquatic ecosystems equilibrium between two main processes will determine the chemical composition of the water system. These two processes are called primary production and remineralisation.

Primary production is a photosynthetic process where the production of organic matter by plants occur under an endothermic reaction in the presence of sunlight (Eq. {ref}`eq_primary_production`). When algae die they settle on the bottom of a lake, decreasing the depth, and are being remineralized. Respiration or remineralisation is the reverse process in which algae are broken down in the presence of oxygen to yield carbon dioxide, water and nutrients under an exothermic reaction (Eq. {ref}`eq_primary_production`). By this process nutrients are recycled from the sediment back to the water by a transport mechanism. Algal remineralisation occurs during both dark and daylight hours.

:::{math}
:label: eq_primary_production
\ce{106 CO2 + 16 HNO3 + H3PO4 + 122 H2O <=> (CH2O)106 (NH3)16 (H3PO4) + 138 O2}
:::

Stoichiometry helps us to determine the ratios of elements assimilated during primary production or respiration. The molar ratio of C:N:P = 106:16:1 is known as the Redfield, Ketchum and Richards (RKR) ratio, and represents the chemical composition of phytoplankton. This ratio is not for all phytoplankton, for example some bacteria can grow in the ratio of N:P equal to 6:7. Some bacteria can grow when the ratio of C:P is equal to 600:1, but they grow very slowly. Fast growing organisms need a lot of phosphorus. In contrast to other ratios, the C:N ratio does not vary a lot from 106:16. Algae assimilate nutrients in these ratios and when algal cells decompose they release nutrients in the same molar ratios, and by that regulating the chemical composition of natural waters.

Carbon, nitrogen and phosphorus are the most important plant macro-nutrients i.e. they are required in relatively large amounts for algal growth. For certain phytoplankton such as diatoms, silicon is also a very important macro-nutrient. Furthermore, common macro- or major nutrients are: C, H, O, P, N and Si (diatoms).

Common micro or minor nutrients are: S, Cl, K, Na, Ca, Mg, Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu, B, Mo, Se, and Co. Even though they are required in lesser amounts they are still necessary and may limit the growth of phytoplankton. For example iron (Fe) and molybdenum (Mo) are important nutrients for nitrogen fixation and their absence will result in nitrogen limitation.

(sec-5-1-2-2)=
### 5.1.2.2. Limiting nutrient

A limiting nutrient is the one that limits the growth of phytoplankton by its absence, i.e. it is present in the smallest amount. Phosphorus is usually a limiting nutrient for algal growth in fresh waters /inland waters while nitrogen is a limiting nutrient in coastal and ocean waters. In seawater both nitrogen and phosphorus can be limiting. For example in the Baltic Sea phosphorus is generally a limiting nutrient. In the Bothnian sea around the Swedish coasts phosphorus is growth limiting, while towards the south nitrogen becomes a limiting nutrient.

Furthermore, not all plants in water need to be limited by the same nutrient. For example blue-green algae can fix nitrogen gas from the atmosphere and therefore tend to be phosphorus limited. In addition, nitrogen and phosphorus are not the only nutrients important for the algal growth. Any of micro- or macro-nutrients could become a limiting factor for algal growth.

Nutrients are not the only factors affecting eutrophied lake ecosystems. Other common limiting factors are light, temperature and stratification.

(sec-5-1-2-3)=
### 5.1.2.3. Biota

After being converted to algae by biological production, nutrients are transported to higher tropic levels through the food chain. A classification of freshwater ecosystems (like lakes) according to trophic levels in a grazing food chain is on primary producers (phytoplankton), primary consumers (zooplankton), secondary consumers (fish) and tertiary consumers (birds). In other words, if one wants to model eutrophication of a lake where for example phosphorus is a limiting nutrient, one should consider not only soluble phosphorus concentrations but also organically bounded phosphorus in all tropic levels, from phytoplankton to fishes.

Furthermore, within each tropic level several species can be found. There are four groups of phytoplankton-algae that are important concerning the eutrophication problem: blue-green algae, green algae, diatoms, and pigmented flagellates. They are all classified according to the color of the organism. Blue-green algae as a group are most abundant in the early fall, while green algae are most abundant in mid-summer. Both groups live near the surface of the water often producing offensive mats or blankets. Diatoms are usually found during the cooler months, they can be found even under the ice. They can grow twice a year due to the spring and autumn destratification and mixing (see {ref}`Section 5.1.3.2. <sec-5-1-3-2>`), in which food is brought to the surface. They live at almost any depth of the water body, both near the surface and in the bottom mud.

The second classification of algae is according to their means of motility: nekton (algae that move-swim with flagella), phytoplankton (free-floating algae which are most commonly found in lakes), and periphyton (attached or benthic algae).

(sec-5-1-3)=
## 5.1.3. Factors affecting eutrophication

The most important factors that influence the growth of algae (or biological production) are light intensity, temperature and stratification, the concentrations and distributions of oxygen, nitrogen, phosphorus and carbon. For carbon cycle description see the carbon cycle example in {ref}`Section 6.1.2. <sec-6-1-2>`.


(sec-5-1-3-1)=
### 5.1.3.1. Light

Important factor that influences the lake productivity is light. Sunlight determines the rate of photosynthesis which determines the rate of production of plants, and therefore the production of animals. The distribution of algae at the various depth of the lake is directly correlated with the intensity of illumination at the respective depths. In the absence of light, plants take up oxygen and reduce the dissolved oxygen concentration of the lake due to respiration. Intensity and duration of sunlight are modeled as a sine function ($t$ in days):

:::{math}
S = S_{\mathrm{max}}(1 + \sin(0.008603t))
:::

Light intensity at any given depth of the lake depends on the light intensity at the water surface, the depth and transparency of the water. Eutrophic lakes reduce transparency and increase turbidity.

In the absence of light respiration dominates, algae take up oxygen and reduce the dissolved oxygen concentration and therefore an anoxic layer within the lake is formed. But there is a maximum growth rate of algae, and with further increasing of light the growth rate decreases (see {ref}`fig_algea_growth_factors`).

:::{figure} ../images/fig_algea_growth_factors.png
:label: fig_algea_growth_factors
:width: 600px
The influence of nutrient concentrations, light intensity and temperature on the algal growth rate (@schnoor1996).
:::

:::{note} Did you know?
Transparency is dependent on the presence of dissolved and suspended matter in the water and the algal concentration. Eutrophication reduces the transparency of a lake and it is measured with Secchi disks. In eutrophic lakes the Secchi depth is usually from few decimeters up to 2 m. In oligotrophic lakes the Secchi depth is usually 7-20 m.
::: 

(sec-5-1-3-2)=
### 5.1.3.2. Temperature and stratification

The higher the temperature of the water the greater is the productivity of the lake. However, the solubility of oxygen in the water decreases with increasing temperature causing the risk for oxygen depletion.

Temperature is influenced by the geographical position of the lake, the time of the year and the discharge of heated effluents (pollution) into the lake. The temperature influences the stratification of lakes. The density of water is temperature dependent and the maximum density occurs at 4°C (hypolimnion). Less densified water will float on top of higher densified water leading to lake stratification.

During the year as water warms or cools seasonally the temperature profile within a lake changes. The water in lakes usually circulates during spring and autumn, and stagnates in summer and winter. During summer and winter months stable stratification develops with three layers (see {ref}`fig_lake_stratification_summer_winter`). This is due to the thermal stratification. The deeper part is called hypolimnion where temperature and density do not significantly change through out the year (temperature is around 4°C with maximum density around 1 000 kg/m{sup}`3`). The surface layer is called epilimnion, and is very dependent on solar radiation, it is warm in the summer and cold in the winter (close to zero degrees). The layer in between is called thermocline where a significant temperature gradient occurs, particularly in the summer.

The mixing of the water is dependent on the temperature gradients, wind velocity and direction, the surface area and the depth of the lake. In autumn the temperature of the epilimnion decreases, the epilimnion becomes denser equalizing with the hypolimnion, the stratification gets destabilized causing a circulation of the water in the lake by wind (see {ref}`fig_lake_stratification_spring_autumn`).

In winter the epilimnion is colder than the hypolimnion but in summer it is the opposite. During winter the bottom waters become anoxic which results in phosphorus release. The mobilization of phosphorus due to reducing conditions in the sediments can be even higher in the summertime, which is mixed into the whole water body during the autumn circulation. The summer stagnation period is important because there is no oxygen input to the hypolimnion, which therefore becomes anoxic. The epilimnion does not receive dissolved nutrients from the bottom and therefore primary productivity becomes nutrient limited, and decreases during the summer.

As shown in {ref}`fig_algea_growth_factors` the algal growth rate increases with increasing temperature of water until it reaches its maximum. Then with further increase of temperature the growth rate starts to decrease. Phosphorus levels are the highest at the end of winter when algal growth is close to minimum because of low temperatures, short days and low light intensity.

(sec-5-1-3-3)=
### 5.1.3.3. Oxygen (vertical profiles)

The concentration of dissolved oxygen in a lake is determined by a balance between oxygen inputs through surface aeration and photosynthesis, and oxygen losses through remineralisation. Increased nutrient load increases primary productivity i.e. the quantity of organic material that needs to be decomposed aerobically. {ref}`fig_effect_dissolved_oxygen_river` shows the effect of organic pollution on a river community. When organic pollutant enters a river a dissolved oxygen concentration decreases along the river stream creating an oxygen sag curve. The shape of the curve will depend on many factors such as the level and a frequency of pollution, flows, temperature, seasonal variations, and the degree of river aeration (@snape1995).

The increasing growth of algae due to eutrophication has the effect on the oxygen balance of natural waters, which can vary a lot during the day. During the daylight hours algae produce oxygen with photosynthesis, and during the hours of darkness algae require oxygen for their metabolic processes. Considerable variations in dissolved oxygen concentrations during the day (from 2 mg/l at night to 13 mg/l during daylight) may be responsible for the disappearance of high quality fish in natural waters.

:::{figure} ../images/fig_effect_dissolved_oxygen_river.png
:label: fig_effect_dissolved_oxygen_river
:width: 400px
Dissolved oxygen profile of a river downstream of a discharge (@snape1995).
:::

(sec-5-1-3-4)=
### 5.1.3.4. Phosphorus cycle in aquatic ecosystems

The primary source of phosphorus is phosphate rock deposits, which are extensively mined each year. Phosphor is a finite source and within 150 years the appetite mines will be empty and we will have no reliable phosphor source left. Phosphor is relatively insoluble, only 10 % of the phosphorus that entered a body of water is in the soluble form available for algal consumption. Nearly all dissolved and particulate forms of P are combined or modified forms of phosphate ion (PO{sub}`4`{sup}`3-`). It is usually found in relatively small concentrations in natural waters and that is why is often a limiting factor. Plants utilize phosphor in the form of orthophosphate. {ref}`fig_P_cycle_aquatic` shows a phosphorus cycle in an aquatic environment. Dissolved organic phosphorus and particulate organic phosphorus make a detritus pool. The highest concentrations of phosphorus are found at the end of winter when algal growth is at minimum owing to decreased temperature and short days.

Detergents are a major anthropogenic source of phosphor to wastewater entering natural waters. It is easily precipitated from solution with ferric ions (Fe{sup}`3+`) in form of FePO{sub}`4`.

:::{figure} ../images/fig_P_cycle_aquatic.png
:label: fig_P_cycle_aquatic
:width: 600px
Phosphorus cycle in aquatic ecosystem (@michigan2006).
:::

(sec-5-1-3-5)=
### 5.1.3.5. Nitrogen cycle in aquatic ecosystem

Nitrogen together with phosphorus is the most important nutrient from an eutrophication point of view, since it increases the primary production in fresh waters and therefore determines the degree of lake eutrophy. Nitrogen exists in natural waters as dissolved nitrogen, ammonium, nitrate, nitrite and in organic compounds. Transformation of nitrogen from one form to the other in water is very dependent on the redox potential and the dissolved oxygen concentration (DO). Atmospheric nitrogen can be fixed by blue-green algae in water and by nitrogen-fixing bacteria in the sediments. With the process called ammonification organic nitrogen compounds are broken down and nitrogen is released in form of ammonium, by nitrification it is oxidized to nitrate (Eq. {ref}`eq_nitrification`), and it is released to the atmosphere by the action of denitrifying bacteria as shown in {ref}`fig_N_cycle_lake_eutro`. At lower DO, in anoxic conditions denitrification occurs according to Eq. {ref}`eq_denitrification`.

:::{math}
:label: eq_nitrification
\ce{NH4+ + 2O2 -> NO3- + 2H+ + H2O}
:::

:::{math}
:label: eq_denitrification
\ce{5CH2O + 4NO3- -> 2N2 + 4HCO3- + CO2 + 3H2O}
:::

:::{figure} ../images/fig_N_cycle_lake_eutro.png
:label: fig_N_cycle_lake_eutro
:width: 400px
Nitrogen cycle in the eutrophic lake (@larsen1977).
:::

The main anthropogenic source for nitrogen is agricultural fertilizer. Nitrates have high solubility and low sorption characteristics so they can be easily infiltrated to the groundwater. The interaction between the surface water and the groundwater is very important because nitrogen concentration in surface waters can be influenced by the concentration of nitrogen from groundwater. A flux from ground water pool to the surface water pool is in the same order of magnitude as the discharge of surface water to the ocean. Furthermore, storage of ground water as well as turnover time is much higher comparing to the surface water pool. Due to long travel time (rate of transport) nitrogen is accumulating over the years, and even if nitrogen concentration would significantly decrease by cutting point and diffuse sources, it will take a long time until we could see the difference in the surface waters.

As phosphor is usually the rate-limiting nutrient, not all the nitrate can be utilized by the algae and thus nitrate contents in lakes have been increasing. The amount of nitrogen in the aquatic environment is important to algae because it determines the amount of chlorophyll-a that may be formed, too much nitrogen inhibits the formation of chlorophyll and limits growth. High nitrate in drinking water (through the groundwater) cause blue baby syndrome (disruption of oxygen transport through the loss of hemoglobin in the blood) and the compound nitrosoamine causes cancer.


(sec-5-1-4)=
## 5.1.4. Fundamental processes

(sec-5-1-4-1)=
### 5.1.4.1. From normal to eutrophied lake (internal sinks and sources)

a) {u}`A normal lake`

As mentioned earlier, eutrophication is a natural process where nutrients are accumulated due to soil erosion or natural fertilization, then converted to algae-phytoplankton in the process called biological production or photosynthesis. In this process nutrients are food for algae. In a food chain phytoplankton is a potential food and energy source for higher organisms –zooplankton, and they are food for fishes. Dead organic matter creates a detritus pool i.e. a sinking organic matter pool, which on its way to the bottom sediment can be remineralized. This is an internal source of nutrients. Other parts of nutrients from the detritus pool that have not been remineralized are deposited in sediments as an internal sink of nutrients (see {ref}`fig_lake_normal`). Remineralisation or bacterial decay is a process versus photosynthesis where nutrients from both the detritus pool and from the sediment are released back to the water via diffusion in the same ratio as taken (the RKR ratio). All processes should last for hundreds or thousands of years. In well-mixed oxygen rich water systems sediments are divided to an upper aerobic part and lower anoxic part, which ensure a high P sink with short P retention times. Due to extensive nitrification a N sink is poor with long N retention times.

:::{figure} ../images/fig_lake_normal.png
:label: fig_lake_normal
:width: 300px
A normal lake.
:::

b) {u}`Addition of nutrients through point/ non point sources`

Human activities are contributing to increased levels of phosphorus and nitrogen in our water systems. Sources of pollution can be through the point and non-point sources. Point source pollution is coming from specific, identifiable locations like untreated or partially treated nutrient rich wastewater. They are more easily controlled by using detergents without phosphor, and can be removed by for example tertiary treatment of wastewater i.e. removing of phosphorus from wastewater with precipitation with FeCl{sub}`3` or FeSO{sub}`4` or with biological treatment.

Non-point ,i.e. diffuse, sources are coming from a wide range of locations like agricultural runoff, storm-water runoff, groundwater, golf-course runoff, and therefore it is more difficult to obtain the reduction of pollution coming from non point sources.

:::{admonition} 📝 **Example: The distribution of point and diffuse sources of phosphorus into a lake Simcoe in USA (@unu2006).**
:class: seealso
:icon: false
Usually point sources are not the major inputs of pollution. On the contrary, non point sources are both difficult to locate and are in abundance. For example an average of 100 tones of phosphorus enter lake Simcoe every year of which: 6% comes from wastewater treatment plants; 25% from urban storm-water runoff; 24% from soil erosion from crops, stream banks and shorelines; 15% runoff from manure piles, livestock access to streams; 2% faulty septic systems; 28% natural inputs. Recently it has been shown that a large percentage of phosphorus input is via atmospheric pathways.
:::

Concentration of contaminants in fresh waters are controlled not only by point and diffuse sources but also with a multitude of biogeochemical reactions that may occur in the water column during the transport.

With addition of nutrients the growth of algae is accelerated, the zooplankton growth is accelerated, more fishes are growing, and more sediment builds up. The depth of the lake decreases, the rate of respiration increases. In total all processes are accelerated (see {ref}`fig_lake_added_nutrients`). Denitrification occurs which increase anoxic part of sediments. Now sediments are a moderate P sink with longer residence times, whereas a N sink is high with short N residence times. Primary production becomes nitrogen limited which creates good environment for nitrogen-fixing bacteria i.e. another internal source increases.

:::{figure} ../images/fig_lake_added_nutrients.png
:label: fig_lake_added_nutrients
:width: 300px
A lake after an extensive addition of nutrients.
:::

c) {u}`An eutrophied lake`

In an eutrophied lake these processes that would have occurred over geological time scales are accelerated to decades. Due to a rich supply of nutrients an algal bloom occurs, which is extremely undesirable because it acts as a barrier to the penetration of oxygen (leads to oxygen depletion) into the water, which may result in fish kills. Since biological production increases rapidly so does a detritus pool that will eventually sediment, decreasing the depth of the lake. The dissolved oxygen in the water is reduced through decay or respiration within the bloom, but also due to biological breakdown of sediments. Since oxygen cannot be mixed vertically a deep water becomes anoxic causing a fish death i.e. a bottom death, which is a big problem in the Baltic Sea. Furthermore, some blooms may also release toxic substances that are capable of killing fish and wild life. Therefore, many native fish species may disappear and be replaced by species more resistant to the new conditions.

:::{note} Did you know?
A bloom is the concentration of plankton that equals or exceeds 500 individual organisms per ml of raw water. Blooms usually show a predominance of blue-green algae although algae from other classes can also form blooms (@pfafflin1983).
::: 

In hypolimnion phosphorus precipitates as iron phosphate (FePO{sub}`4`) under aerobic conditions. During decomposition of sediments, in aerobic conditions, microorganisms consume oxygen to reduce organic material. Ferric hydroxide (Fe(OH){sub}`3`) shows a very high phosphate adsorption capacity. When oxygen is lacking, in anoxic conditions, FePO{sub}`4` and Fe(OH){sub}`3` are used for mineralization as an oxygen source. Ferri ions Fe{sup}`3+` are reduced to ferro Fe{sup}`2+` ions and phosphorus release occurs. This is a process responsible for internal phosphorus loading!! When hydroxide is used up, methanogenic bacteria take over and production of methane, hydrogen sulphide and ammonia occur due to anaerobic decomposition of sediments. Furthermore, sulphuric acid is reduced to H{sub}`2`S according to Eq. {ref}`eq_reduction_S_acid`:

:::{math}
:label: eq_reduction_S_acid 
\ce{2CH2O + SO4^2- -> H2S + 2HCO3-}
:::

Hydrogen sulphide will react with iron according to Eq. {ref}`eq_precipitation_FeS` and will precipitate as FeS.

:::{math}
:label: eq_precipitation_FeS
\ce{Fe^2+ + H2S -> FeS + 2H+}
:::

In the eutrophied lake sediments are completely anoxic and due to the concentration gradient where there is no nitrate in an anoxic zone no denitrification occurs (see {ref}`fig_lake_eutrophied`). Therefore accumulation of nitrogen occurs and an internal sink of N is lost. In these reducing conditions phosphate is released. Increased phosphorus concentration increases algae production, increases detritus pool, increase sediments, which results in even larger internal loading of P. The bottom sediments have become P and N sources instead of sinks. Residence times of P and N are long, and primary production is limited by light.

The phosphorus can be mobilized from the sediments (internal loading) to even greater extent comparing with the phosphorus loading from point or non point sources (external loading). For example, lake Finjasjön in Scania receives 4 tons of phosphorus via streams but exports 10 tons. The excess 6 tons of phosphorus are mobilized from the sediments that have become an internal source.

:::{figure} ../images/fig_lake_eutrophied.png
:label: fig_lake_eutrophied
:width: 300px
An eutrophied lake.
:::

(sec-5-1-5)=
## 5.1.5 Consequences of eutrophication

When eutrophication occurs, aquatic ecosystems suffer many ecological consequences. Common results of eutrophication are increased plant (algal bloom) and animal masses, and bacterial populations (overgrowth), which cause an increase in an organic detritus pool. Due to oxygen depletion the formation of anoxic zones in deep bottoms occurs and therefore the formation of hydrogen sulphide, methane and ammonia occur too. These products are toxic to most higher organisms. Due to previously mentioned processes mobilization of phosphorus and nitrogen from the bottom sediments occurs (sediments became a source instead of sink). Furthermore, eutrophied waters have increased turbidity (reduction in transparency to light as shown in {ref}`fig_photo_eutro_lake`) and the rate of sedimentation. All this result in decreased diversity due to death of bottom fauna and fish. The structure of species is changed and the balance of the ecosystems is disturbed. Less desirable fish species dominate like Cyprinid fish as shown in {ref}`fig_consequences_lakefauna_eutro`.

:::{figure} ../images/fig_consequences_lakefauna_eutro.png
:label: fig_consequences_lakefauna_eutro
:width: 300px
Changes in lakes fauna (@kiely1997).
:::

Eutrophication has also negative effects on humans. Due to nutrient enrichment toxic algal strains are produced. Decaying of dead plants and algae can produce bad odors and tastes of the water. They can develop into an unsightly scum along the water surface decreasing recreational resources (for swimming and sailing) and reducing a sight depth (see {ref}`fig_photo_toxic_algal`). When the water becomes turbulent algae may enter a water system clogging the irrigation channels. In the water treatment further treatment is required for industrial use. Eutrophication has also long-term effects on fishing resources.

(sec-5-1-6)=
## 5.1.6 Prevention and restoration of eutrophication

There are a number of methods that can be used to reduce and control the plant growth. They can be grouped as nutrient prevention techniques and methods that can restore an already eutrophied lake.

The prevention and control of nutrient release into water bodies is the most important part if eutrophication is to be decelerated. To reduce nutrient loading tertiary treatment (reduction of phosphorus and nitrogen) in wastewater treatment plants (WWTP) should be introduced. One example is Lake Washington, {ref}`fig_lake_recovery`, where it is shown how fast nutrient balance can be reached by introducing WWTP in the area. Phosphate loading can be reduced by using detergents without phosphorus, by controlling fertilization, etc.

:::{figure} ../images/fig_lake_recovery.png
:label: fig_lake_recovery
:width: 400px
Recovery of lake Washington (@kiely1997).
:::

After ensuring pollution prevention, further step is to restore the eutrophied lake. Depending on the specific lake conditions and the degree of lake eutrophy, the choice of a proper restoration technique has to be made. Desludging or removing of bottom sediments can remove an internal source of nutrients and therefore decrease food for algae. This is a very expensive method, but there is a possibility to decrease costs by selling the sludge as a fertilizer. Harvesting of algae, aquatic weeds and fish can remove the biomass in the lake that will eventually produce more nutrients and reduce more oxygen. Diluting of polluted water with fresh water with lower concentration of nutrients can be one of options for some lakes. Another restoration technique is destratification or artificially induced circulation of the water by introducing compressed air (surface aeration) or pumping warm oxygen rich water to the bottom of the lake (deep-level aeration). With this technique oxygen is distributed to all parts of the water body and anoxic layers disappear.

Another group of methods is chemical addition where either precipitation or oxidation occurs. In lakes Dlugie in Poland and Kirkkojärvi in Finland PAX (polyaluminium chloride) has been added in order to precipitate phosphorus and therefore restore dying lakes (see Eq. {ref}`eq_lake_recover_PAX`). During PAX treatments the acidity of the lake increased slightly and returned to its natural level. Furthermore, the reduction of trash fish like Cyprinid fish occurred due to its high sensitivity to acidification.

:::{math}
:label: eq_lake_recover_PAX
\ce{AlCl3 + PO4^3- <=> AlPO4 + 3Cl-}
:::

Further chemical treatment is the introduction of NaNO{sub}`3` to natural waters that removes H{sub}`2`S by means of oxidation to sulphate according to Eq. {ref}`eq_lake_recover_NaNO3`

:::{math}
:label: eq_lake_recover_NaNO3
\ce{2NaNO3 + H2S -> N2 + Na2SO4 + H2O}
:::
